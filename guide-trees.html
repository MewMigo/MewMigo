CAT GUIDE

How to Choose the Right Cat Tree for Your Cat

A cat tree can be much more than a piece of furniture tucked into the corner of your home. For the right cat, it can become a favorite sleeping spot, a lookout tower, a scratching station, a place to play, and a comfortable retreat when the world gets a little too busy.

But the best cat tree isn't necessarily the biggest, tallest, or most expensive one you can find.

The right choice depends on your cat, your home, and how your cat actually likes to live.

Age, body size, mobility, personality, the number of cats in your household, and the amount of available space can all influence which cat tree makes sense.

Start With Your Cat's Age

One of the first things to consider is your cat's stage of life.

Kittens

Kittens tend to treat everything as an adventure. They may climb, jump, scratch, chase toys, and explore every level of a cat tree.

A kitten-friendly tree can include multiple platforms, scratching surfaces, ramps, tunnels, and play areas. However, stability is especially important. A wobbly structure can become frightening and could potentially cause an injury when an energetic kitten launches itself onto a platform.

Look for a sturdy design with securely attached components and appropriately sized platforms. Hanging toys should also be checked regularly for loose pieces, strings, or other components that could become chewing or entanglement hazards.

You don't necessarily need to buy the largest tree available. A kitten may eventually outgrow a very small tree, but an enormous tower isn't automatically better.

Adult Cats

Adult cats often give you more clues about what they actually want.

Some love climbing and prefer a tall tree with several elevated platforms. Others would rather have a comfortable perch near a window where they can watch birds, people, or whatever else catches their attention.

For an adult cat, think about their personality as much as their age.

Does your cat climb? Scratch? Hide? Sleep in high places? Watch windows? Play aggressively?

The answers can help determine which features deserve your attention.

Also consider your cat's size and weight. A large cat needs platforms that provide enough room to comfortably stand, turn around, stretch, and rest. The entire structure should be sturdy enough to remain stable during climbing and jumping.

Senior Cats

Senior cats deserve special consideration.

A tall cat tree with widely spaced platforms might have been perfect when your cat was younger, but jumping between levels can become more difficult as cats age or develop mobility limitations.

For older cats, look for designs with lower resting areas, wider platforms, nonslip surfaces, and easier access between levels. Ramps or closely spaced steps can make climbing more manageable.

You don't necessarily need to take away your senior cat's ability to climb. Instead, consider making the different levels easier and safer to reach.

Think About the Space You Actually Have

Before buying a cat tree, measure the area where you intend to put it.

This sounds obvious, but cat trees can look surprisingly different in a room than they do in a product photograph.

For a small apartment or bedroom, a vertical design with a relatively small footprint can provide your cat with valuable climbing and resting space without taking over the floor. If you're ready to compare options, you can browse MewMigo's cat furniture collection.

If you have more room, a wider cat tree can provide multiple resting areas and more opportunities for cats to move around one another.

And don't forget about height.

A tall cat tree may be perfect near a window or in a room with high ceilings, but it needs to be appropriately stable for its height. Follow the manufacturer's installation instructions, and if the design provides an approved method for anchoring the tree, use it when appropriate.

Think about the surrounding space, too. Your cat should have enough room to approach and leave the tree without being forced into awkward jumps or blocked pathways.

A Cat Tree Can Support Natural Behaviors

One of the biggest reasons to consider a cat tree is that it can give cats opportunities to perform behaviors that come naturally to them.

Climbing and elevated resting can give cats a place to observe their surroundings. Elevated areas can also give cats greater control over their environment, which can be particularly valuable in multi-cat households.

Scratching surfaces provide an appropriate place for a normal feline behavior while also allowing cats to deposit scent from glands in their paws.

Enclosed spaces and hideaways can give a cat somewhere to retreat when it wants privacy or feels uncomfortable.

Play platforms and toys can encourage opportunities for activity, exploration, and predatory play. For more ideas, explore MewMigo's toys and enrichment section.

A thoughtfully designed cat tree can therefore bring together several useful opportunities for climbing, scratching, resting, hiding, and exploration in one piece of furniture.

That doesn't mean a cat tree replaces exercise, playtime, appropriate scratching areas, or other environmental resources. Think of it as one part of a larger environment designed around your cat's needs.

One Cat or Multiple Cats?

The number of cats in your household should also influence your decision.

A single cat may be perfectly happy with one favorite perch.

A multi-cat household can be more complicated.

If several cats want to use the same elevated sleeping area, competition can develop. A larger cat tree with multiple platforms can give cats more options to spread out, but one enormous tree isn't necessarily the answer.

Depending on the personalities and relationships between your cats, it may be better to provide multiple climbing and resting locations throughout the home.

Look for designs that allow cats to move around one another without forcing them into the same space. If a cat is able to enter a hiding area but another cat can easily block the only exit, that design may not provide the sense of security you're looking for.

Don't Underestimate the Design

Cat trees come in an enormous variety of designs.

There are minimalist trees designed to blend into modern homes, elaborate towers with tunnels and hammocks, wall-mounted climbing systems, compact scratching towers, window-oriented perches, and everything in between.

The important question isn't simply:

"Which one looks best?"

Instead, ask:

"Which design gives my cat the things they actually enjoy?"

A beautiful cat tree with a tiny sleeping platform won't be particularly useful if your cat loves stretching out.

A huge tower covered in toys may be wasted on a cat who primarily wants a quiet place to nap beside the window.

And a small scratching post may be more useful than a giant tower if scratching is your cat's favorite activity.

Pay attention to the materials, too.

Look for sturdy construction, securely attached platforms, appropriate scratching materials, and surfaces that are practical to clean. You can browse cat furniture and scratching options to compare different styles.

Removable or washable bedding can be especially helpful. Cats shed, track litter, occasionally have accidents, and generally have a talent for getting things dirty in ways their humans never anticipated.

A cat tree that is difficult to clean may eventually become less appealing to both you and your cat.

Consider Your Cat's Health and Mobility

A cat tree shouldn't be viewed as a medical device, but the right design can support an environment that encourages movement and gives your cat opportunities to climb, stretch, play, rest, and observe.

For healthy cats, different heights and platforms can encourage movement throughout the day.

For cats with reduced mobility, however, accessibility becomes much more important.

A senior cat may still want to reach a favorite elevated resting place but may no longer be comfortable making a large jump to get there. A design with ramps, steps, lower platforms, or closely spaced levels may make that space more accessible.

If your cat has a known mobility problem, pain, or other medical condition, discuss appropriate activity and environmental modifications with your veterinarian rather than assuming a particular cat tree will be suitable.

How to Encourage Your Cat to Use a New Cat Tree

You've found the perfect cat tree, assembled it, and placed it in the ideal location.

Then your cat looks at it for three seconds and walks away.

Don't panic.

And, importantly, don't force your cat onto the tower.

Picking up your cat and placing them on a cat tree may seem like a harmless way to show them what you've bought, but if the experience makes your cat uncomfortable, you could actually make them less interested in the new object. Give your cat an opportunity to discover the tower on their own.

Try placing a favorite blanket, familiar bedding, or favorite toy on one of the platforms. Familiar scents can help make a new object feel less unfamiliar. If you're still looking for a tree, see the cat furniture currently featured on MewMigo.

You can also use play to introduce the tower naturally. Try playing with your cat's favorite wand or interactive toy near the tree, gradually moving the toy toward the tower and eventually onto an accessible platform.

If your cat climbs onto the tower and scratches it during play, that's perfectly fine. Scratching is a normal behavior and allows cats to leave scent from glands in their paws as they interact with their environment. This can help make the tree familiar and may increase its appeal over time.

The goal isn't to force your cat to use the tower.

The goal is to make the tower a place your cat chooses to visit.

Some cats will investigate immediately. Others may need several days—or longer—to decide that the strange new piece of furniture belongs in their territory.

Let your cat set the pace.

The MewMigo Take

Choosing a cat tree is really about creating more usable space for your cat within the space you already have.

For kittens, prioritize stability, age-appropriate climbing opportunities, and safe exploration.

For healthy adult cats, consider their personality, climbing habits, body size, and preferred resting locations.

For seniors, accessibility, comfortable platforms, and easier routes between levels may become more important.

A cat who is hesitant around new things? Give them time and let curiosity do the work.

And above all, remember that your cat doesn't care how impressive the cat tree looks in your living room.

They care whether it feels safe, comfortable, interesting, and useful.

The best cat tree isn't necessarily the one with the most platforms or the highest price tag.

It's the one that fits your home and gives your cat more opportunities to live like a cat.
